---

The Earth in the year 3126 is barely comparable to the world once known. Human activity, the rising concentration of carbon dioxide and methane, and the long burning of fossil fuels tore the ozone layer almost completely apart and drove a runaway warming. The ice melted entirely. The former seven continents no longer exist; in their place lies a single global ocean, broken only here and there by the bare peaks of old mountain ranges that still rise a few metres above the water.

The surface is hostile in several ways at once. With the ozone all but gone, ultraviolet light and cosmic radiation reach the surface almost unfiltered, and the planet's weakened magnetic field no longer deflects the sun's outbursts, so solar storms bring sudden, lethal spikes. The old industrial smog never cleared — a persistent photochemical haze still hangs over the water, toxic and self-renewing. An unprotected body on the surface takes a lethal dose within a few hours.

So life withdrew downward. Long ago, humans descended from fish that once left the ocean for the land; now that same lineage has returned to the water. Species that once lived in shallow, sunlit seas keep to the shaded middle depths; creatures of the deep rise to meet them. Geothermal vents on the ocean floor, linked by warm currents, hold the most stable and habitable conditions, and the stations cluster around them.

The ocean is never still. Currents driven by the sun's uneven heat and the pull of the Moon keep the whole water column in slow, constant motion. That movement is carried through the metal-and-mineral hulls of the stations as a low, continuous groaning that rises and falls without pause. The inhabitants call it the breathing of the world. Without a visible sun, days are not governed by light but by the mechanical pulse of the stations — the cycles of pressure adjustment, oxygen renewal, and filtration. Days are called cycles, divided into numbered intervals. Discrepancies in the logs are common: sometimes an interval seems skipped, sometimes one repeats. Engineers attribute it to clerical error.

## WILDLIFE

This is an age of marine life. The few species that survived the collapse now meet nearly optimal conditions, and populations have grown quickly; many animals reach at least twice the size of their ancestors. Terrestrial life has all but vanished — reports of surviving birds are treated as unverified, the stuff of salvagers' stories.

Cold-water and deep-water species dominate: icefish and toothfish that once kept to the poles and the abyss have spread up into the shaded middle depths. A vast proliferation of krill anchors a stable food web. The total biomass of whales now exceeds that of the remaining human population; their songs are audible at every depth, and people in the lower modules describe a constant sense of being listened to by something vast and unseen.

Gigantism is everywhere. Sea spiders that once reached seventy centimetres now grow to many times that. Sponges form vast, forest-like structures across the floor, and salvagers move through them carefully — their geometries are described as disorienting, almost recursive, the same formation repeating with small variations across kilometres. A few large animals still brave the open: some seals and walruses have grown radiation-resistant skin and haul out on the bare island-peaks for days. Rarer still are animals whose hide gives near-complete protection from the sun; they are almost never seen, but their tracks are sometimes found on the rock above the stations. The tracks, when measured, are always exactly the same size.

Deep-sea fish increasingly move up into the middle depths. One is the so-called Sea Devil, an anglerfish now found at relatively shallow depths. Kept specimens light the lower modules with their bioluminescent lures. Their pulses are perfectly regular — once every 4.7 seconds. Wild specimens, salvagers report, pulse irregularly. No one in the stations remarks on the difference.

## HUMANS

No one knows exactly how many people survive — perhaps a few dozen stations worldwide and a total population under a hundred thousand. Life takes place almost entirely inside enclosed, colony-like underwater habitats. Surface excursions happen only at need and only in protective gear, and even then a solar storm can turn a survivable window lethal without warning.

Centuries underwater have changed the body. Skin has paled; eyes have grown larger; fingers and toes are slightly elongated. Children born in the deeper modules often have a faint bluish translucence to the skin, veins visible beneath. Vitamin D deficiency is universal and bone density runs low — which is exactly why light is treated as medicine, and why time in the Solar Space is part of staying alive (see *The Solar Space*). The average lifespan is around fifty-five years. Aging is demanding, but it is met communally and without shame; the elderly are deeply respected as the holders of the stations' memory, and the Memory Keepers are venerated as living archives.

Children are rare and raised communally; biological parentage is acknowledged but not emphasised, because the bond between a child and the whole station is held to matter more. Some children born in the same cycle share gestures with one another — the same tilt of the head when listening, the same movement of the hands when they speak. Mothers find it endearing. Memory Keepers find it familiar.

## SOCIAL STRUCTURE

The stations are organised around Councils of Mothers — assemblies of elder women who hold both the institutional memory and the final decision-making authority. The system arose in the early centuries underwater, when long-term planning and careful resource management became essential. Power is deliberately distributed: each Council holds several Mothers, each with a portfolio, so that no single system and no single person is ever the only point of failure. Decisions weigh immediate needs against long-term reserves.

The resulting society is, for all the hardness of its conditions, broadly peaceful and cooperative. Community is the highest value; people are raised to be solution-oriented and ready to compromise, because survival has always depended on it. Assemblies are genuinely open gatherings, and the work of keeping everyone alive is shared.

Space inside a station is vertical, but it is not a class system. Modules nearer the surface sit closer to the filtered light and to the Solar Space; the deeper modules are darker and colder. This nearness to light is treated as a practical and a sacred fact — the surface is where the dangerous, life-giving light comes from — not as a measure of a person's worth. People move between levels freely, and the deeper modules are not a place of exile but simply a place that is dark, where the songs and the bioluminescent markers matter most for finding one's way.

A loose Confederacy of stations exists, maintained by periodic submarine visits along the warm currents. Trade is small but symbolically vital: salvaged metals, rare seeds, preserved books, medical supplies. Each visit is also an occasion for marriages, for the exchange of Memory Keepers, and for the renewal of a shared identity — without these visits, the stations would drift into separate civilisations. There is a cautionary story, passed between stations, of a distant station where a single Mother is said to have dissolved her Council and ruled alone; no envoy has returned from there in over a decade. Whether it is true or not, it is told as a warning of what happens when power stops being shared.

## CULTURAL LIFE

### Art

Art is communal and made from whatever can be salvaged — pigments from algae and rust, fragments of metal and glass, worn objects given a second life. Its sensibility owes much to what an old salvaged text calls the praise of shadows: beauty is found not in brightness or polish but in simplicity, in patina, in the gradations of the dark. The inhabitants do not fight the dimness of their world; they have learned to find its beauty, in the soft glow of a lure, the sheen of a worn handrail, the deep tone of a bowl in low light. Murals on the bulkheads return again and again to the ocean, the Moon, the distant Sun, and the light itself.

### Music

Music is communal and almost everywhere. A few salvaged instruments survive, but most music is unaccompanied: call-and-response work songs, lullabies, choral singing.

The most distinctive form is woven into the architecture of daily life. Different areas of a station carry their own songs — specific melodies that belong to specific places. People sing or hum them more or less constantly, together, breaking off only to speak or to do work that makes singing impossible. Because each area has its own song, the sound itself tells you where you are: in the dark, you navigate by the song around you as much as by touch or by the bioluminescent markers. This is not about rank or status — it marks place, not person. And because everyone is always, quietly, singing the same thing in the same place, the practice binds the community together more tightly than almost anything else. The older percussive Hull Song — rhythms struck against the walls, different sections answering each other across the modules — still travels the whole height of a station and remains one of the few forms that connects every level at once.

### Daily Rituals

Mechanical cycles govern the day, but small rituals soften the rhythm. On waking, people press a hand briefly to the hull — the Morning Touch, an acknowledgement of the structure that keeps them alive. Eating alone is considered shameful; even in the deepest modules, meals are shared. At the close of each cycle, in many stations, a Memory Keeper asks aloud, "Is everyone still here?", and the community answers with a count. The count has never come up short. It has, on rare occasions, come up over. And during the deepest part of the rest-cycle there are the Silence Hours, when all non-essential sound is set aside — even the songs stop — and the station is allowed to breathe alone.

### Birth and Death

Birth is ceremonial. A newborn is presented first to the Council of Mothers and then to the whole station, and is given a name drawn from the salvaged pre-flood archives — names of cities, of animals, of stars. Death is returned to the ocean in a rite called The Descent: the body is weighted and released from an airlock as family and close companions gather, while a Memory Keeper recites the contributions of the dead. The body sinks into the dark. It is believed that the dead become part of the ocean's memory, joining the whales and the deep currents.

## TECHNOLOGY

Technological progress has been at a near standstill for centuries. Most systems date from before the flood and have been crudely adapted for underwater use; innovation has largely been replaced by maintenance — the art of keeping ancient machines alive past their intended lives. What new development there is tends to be small, slow, and mechanical.

Light comes from bioluminescent material and from living animals, above all the captured Sea Devils, whose cages are carefully tended and whose feeding is ritualised. Air is produced by a combination of legacy electrolysis units and CO₂ scrubbers together with preserved plants grown in hydroponic compartments; the system is precarious, and more than one station has been lost over the centuries to oxygen failure. Salvager crews descend to the Drowned Cities in submersibles to recover usable components from the wreckage — dangerous, prestigious work, and the source of strange reports: ruins too intact for their age, devices labelled in unreadable scripts, the occasional system still drawing power from a source no one can identify.

But the deepest shift in the stations' technology is a turn back toward the biological. Increasingly, the materials of daily life are grown rather than salvaged. The primary building material is now **Biorock** — also called Seacrete or Seament — a mineral that accretes onto metal frames passed through with a low electric current in seawater, slowly growing a hard, stone-like, self-repairing structure. Stations are no longer only built; in part, they are cultivated, and a damaged wall can be coaxed to heal itself. The Solar Space is the great exception: it is built of pumice — light, porous, volcanic stone that floats — which ties it to the geothermal, volcanic ground on which the whole world depends, and lets it ride at the surface where the light is.

## POLITICS

Each station is governed by a Council of Mothers, an assembly of elder women holding institutional memory and decision-making authority. A Council typically holds five Mothers, each responsible for a distinct domain, who coordinate the systems the station depends on. Power is shared by design, so that responsibility never rests on one person alone.

The **Provisioner Mother** manages food and water — harvesting, cultivation, storage, and the distribution of rations. The **Engineer Mother** oversees the mechanical systems: oxygen, filtration, pressure, and the upkeep of the modules (and, increasingly, the cultivation of their Biorock). The **Memory Mother** holds the histories, maintains the archives, trains the Memory Keepers, and names the newborns from the salvaged archives. The **Navigator Mother** manages relations with other stations — the submarine visits, the trade, the exchange of Memory Keepers. The **Watcher Mother** oversees the Solar Space and the management of light: she maintains the solar instrument with the Shaft Keepers, measures the incoming radiation, and allocates the light where it is needed — for health and for ceremony — making sure that no one receives too much. Because light is both a necessity and a danger, hers is a role of care as much as of authority.

Decisions affecting the whole station are made in open Assemblies, where the Mothers present matters of provisioning, maintenance, travel, and planning, and where the immediate needs of the modules are balanced against the longer management of reserves.

## RELATIONSHIP TO THE SUN

The sun is the defining force of this world, and it is two things at once. It is destructive: its unfiltered radiation is lethal at the surface, its storms strike without warning, and the smog it shines through is itself toxic. And it is the source of all life: nothing lives without its light. The body knows this directly — without light the bones soften, the skin fails, the mind darkens. Light is read, plainly and religiously, as life and as wellbeing.

So the whole culture is organised around a single problem: how to receive just enough of the dangerous light to live, and never more. The Sun is understood as a divine power — creator and destroyer both — who once turned away from humanity, and whose favour must be earned back. Her counterpart is the Moon, the patient protector, who tempers the worst of her light, steadies the tides, and is believed to plead with the Sun on humanity's behalf. The present state of the world is read as a separation between the two, with humanity caught between and trying to mend it.

Rituals take place wherever light enters the station, above all in the Solar Space. They are quiet and measured, because the danger is real regardless of belief: the danger is *too much* light. Not everyone accepts the religious reading — some see the sun as a physical fact and the rites as culture — but all are shaped by the same condition, and all need the light to live.

## THE OFFERING TO THE MOON

Many believe the Moon's protection holds only as long as he is honoured. On the nights of the high tides — when the Moon draws the water column thickest and the coming light will be safest — communities send small offerings drifting up toward the surface: woven cloths, light tokens, things made by hand. It is a quiet, voluntary devotion, open to anyone who wishes to give thanks for the light they receive and to ask the Moon to keep tempering the Sun. The rite stands apart from the practical work of the Council; it is simply an exchange between the people and the Moon, conducted in cloth and salt water beneath the registers the station officially keeps.

## THE SOLAR SPACE — THE EYE OF KHEIR

The Solar Space is the ceremonial and life-sustaining heart of a station — the room where the religion is mainly lived, and where people come to receive light. Unlike the rest of the station, it floats: a body of pumice riding half above and half below the waterline, raised as near the surface as anything inhabited can be, so that the path the light must travel is short.

At its centre is the Eye, the solar instrument. A long, narrow shaft rises from the core of the room, breaks the surface, and ends in a collector that gathers sunlight; inside the shaft, a chain of filtering elements (the original scientific apparatus) attenuates the radiation as it descends, so that the light reaching the room is gentle and safe. It pools at the centre, over an altar, and emerges most directly through two forward-leaning viewing tubes — so that those who wish to take the light most fully lean in toward it.

The Eye was, and still is, an instrument: it measures the sun's radiation, tests what is tolerable, and channels filtered light into the station. Over centuries that function became inseparable from care and from worship. The light it delivers is medicine and blessing at once. People come to sit in it — the ailing, the newly born, the elderly, anyone worn thin by the long dark — because the body needs light to keep its bones and its spirit. To live in the light, even briefly, is to be made well.

And yet the light is never without danger. The shaft is a thinning of the station's shielding; take too much and it harms. So the reception of light is measured — timed and allocated by the Watcher Mother, kept and cleaned by the Shaft Keepers. A single old phrase is preserved without alteration and is taught to every child: *do not look directly at it.* It is not a prohibition on the sacred but a rule of safety — do not take more of the dangerous light than your share.

At certain cosmic constellations the Solar Space comes fully into its own. Above all, once a year, on the eighteenth day of the fourth month, the Moon's tide reaches its annual peak and the water column above the room thickens enough to soften the radiation by natural means; combined with the instrument's own filters, this is the one time when the fullest light can be received in safety. On that day the whole community gathers for the great reception of light — a celebration of life, of survival, of the Moon's continued mercy. Those most in need, and those the station wishes to honour, are given the deepest light. It is said that those who sit long in it are changed: calmer, somehow luminous, slower to speak — they pause for about 4.7 seconds before answering simple questions, as if still listening to something the rest cannot hear.

## WORLD Q&A

**How do people move around in this future?**
Between stations, in small submarine-like vehicles that travel along the warm currents — the most stable routes, and safely below the irradiated surface waters.

**Is long-distance travel still possible?**
Technically yes, but it is rare — expensive, slow, and risky. Only a few have the resources or the need.

**What materials are available for construction, and how are they made?**
Three sources. Salvage from the seafloor remnants of the old civilisation, recovered by submersible crews. Grown material — Biorock (Seacrete/Seament), a mineral accreted onto electrified metal frames in seawater, hard and self-repairing, now the primary way stations are built and mended. And increasingly other biological materials, as the stations' technology bends back toward nature. The Solar Space is the exception, built of floating pumice from the world's volcanic ground.

**What is the relationship between humans and non-humans?**
Close and dependent. Cold-water species are the basis of survival, and some are used directly — bioluminescent fish for light above all. There is no traditional livestock; people rely almost entirely on marine life.

**What does aging feel like in this world?**
Demanding — low light and thin nutrition take their toll — but it is met with care, not shame. Experience is valued, and the old hold the memory the young depend on.

**What is the daily rhythm of life?**
The sun no longer sets the day; artificial light and the mechanical cycles of the station do, woven through with the constant, place-marking songs. Light is tied to health and ceremony, not to status.

**What do people eat?**
Mostly fish, algae, and other marine resources, from harvesting and limited cultivation within the stations.

**How do people relate to the sun?**
It is both feared and revered — dangerous and necessary at once. Direct, prolonged exposure is avoided; measured light is sought out, because the body needs it. Culturally, the sun has become a mythological force, and the careful reception of its light the centre of religious life.

**What do people work at?**
Survival, shared out: maintaining systems, cultivating Biorock and food, salvaging materials, tending the Sea Devils and the Solar Space. Every role keeps the station alive.

## INTRODUCTION STORY

I woke again today to the sound of Number 39 — though down here in Module 7656 there is no real difference between waking and sleeping. Only the faint gold that drips down through the upper light shafts marks anything like time, and the songs: the low one that belongs to our part of the station, which someone is always humming, so you always know where you are even with your eyes shut.

The shell of our module hasn't been properly soundproof for years; repairing it isn't worth the cost, they say, so every groan of Number 39 seeps through. Number 39 is a Sea Devil. They usually live around twenty-five years; this one is nearly thirty and very sick. Every cycle, before Alex goes out to collect algae, he stops to check on it. There's nothing to do but wait. There are more of them now than before — they are the best light we have at this depth — so more people go out to catch them. Hard work, but good work.

Some say the sun is a goddess who turned away from us, and they send offerings up to the Moon to soften her. Alex says there is no sun god — he read it in an old book, that the sun is just a star, a source of energy, and that what people believe now isn't real. I think he's talking nonsense. He's strange. I like him. He gets on well with the old people.

Breakfast was the same as always, algae paste with dried fish. It isn't bad, but every time I eat it I think of foods I've never had. They say there used to be fruit. Sweet. I can't even imagine it. Or bread, made from a plant the colour of the sun. Sometimes, repairing the higher modules, I pass old storerooms marked "Wheat." Empty for years.

Today it was my turn at the oxygen filters. One of the lower modules lost pressure again — it happens; the systems are old, some built before the flood, kept running with patches. Old Jens was there. While I worked he said, "Once, people didn't have to work just to breathe." I laughed, mostly because I didn't know what else to do.

In the afternoon a team came back from outside, one of them hurt — a solar storm had driven the radiation up on their route and forced a long detour; their air nearly ran out. But they brought something back: an old lamp, still working, its light white, not gold like ours. For a few minutes everyone gathered around it. It felt like looking at a piece of another world.

What I want, more than anything, is to go up to the Solar Space when it is my turn and sit in the full light — real light, not this filtered glow that drips down through pipes. They say it makes you well. They say you come back changed. Most of us have bone trouble; I do too — rickets, they call it, too little light. The light up there is supposed to be the cure, doled out carefully so no one takes too much. I think about it more than I should.

Alex once told me something a data-vault guard told him: an old recording, a thousand years old, about people who made too much carbon and methane, who tore the sky, who heated the world until the ice was gone and the radiation came and drove everyone underwater. Alive or dead, he said, they were all coming down here in the end. And now here we are. Not fully alive, not fully gone. At least not in Module 7656 — beneath layers of water, caught in routine, living hour by hour on the little light that reaches us from above. Maybe one cycle, something will change.
